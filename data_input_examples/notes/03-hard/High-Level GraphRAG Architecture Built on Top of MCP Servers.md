## Linking Memory to Context via Knowledge Graphs and Ontologies

After setup and brief introductions, Abi welcomed Paul to the session, noting that he is an AI engineer and author of the bestselling book **LLM Engineering**, and invited students to ask questions throughout the presentation.

Paul began by expressing his excitement about the lecture. He explained that most of his work centers on orchestration and building applications around **LLMs**, rather than specializing in serving or inference. He mentioned that his team had also recently released a self-paced course called **Agentic Engineering**, which he would share a link to at the end.

The presentation, titled "Linking Memory to Context via Knowledge Graphs and Ontologies," is essentially about **GraphRAG**. Paul asked whether he should explain what **RAG** is or assume the audience already knew. Abi suggested that most students would know RAG, but many might not have used GraphRAG directly. Paul confirmed the presentation would build up a full understanding gradually, though familiarity with RAG would help.

Paul started with the problem statement. All modern AI applications and agents suffer from two big issues. The first is **context rot**, which means that a lot of noise accumulates in the context window. When the context window is full or close to full, the ratio between signal and noise becomes very low, and the LLM starts to have serious issues. You need to apply all kinds of context engineering mechanisms to keep the context window in check and the signal high. This affects not just performance, but also cost and latency, because you are processing more tokens. The bottom line is that you want to keep your context window as free as possible, filled only with important information. Related to this is the **lost-in-the-middle** problem, where LLMs tend to be biased toward paying more attention to the beginning and end of the context window. While frontier models may eventually solve this particular bias, the overall context rot problem will likely stick around for a while.

The second core issue is **data fragmentation**. Data gets siloed across multiple databases, services, and platforms. Especially in the era of agents, data lives in so many places: emails, personal notes, articles, messages, databases, documentation. Everything is scattered, and you rarely sit in the beautiful situation where all your data is in one database with a clean API to query it. These are the two core issues that GraphRAG addresses.

Throughout the presentation, Paul used the example of building a **digital twin**, which is very similar to a modern personal assistant. The idea is to build a system on top of your personal notes, research, emails, messages, and whatever else you want to include.

At the core of GraphRAG are **knowledge graphs** and **ontologies**. Paul emphasized that the story really starts from the ontology. For people coming from object-oriented programming, an ontology can be intuitively understood as a collection of classes and how they interact with each other. In the digital twin example, the core entities include **document**, **person**, **task**, **episode**, and **preference**. These are specific types of nodes that can interact with each other through specific relationships. For instance, a person can be mentioned in a document, a person can be connected to another person, a person can have a task, or a person can experience an episode. There is also a relationship between a document and a task, which is optional, but useful for adding lineage, for example, indicating that a task was extracted from a particular document. The ontology is flexible and should be defined according to what makes sense for your specific business use case.

Abi jumped in to give the class additional context. She explained that whenever you need to connect dots across a corpus of multiple documents, rather than just finding the most relevant paragraph, you go for GraphRAG. In a graph-based system, all your knowledge is stored as entities and edges, and you traverse connections rather than just finding similar text. You are trying to find the relationships between all of these entities.

Paul continued, explaining how this ontology gets transformed into an actual knowledge graph. You have your data across multiple documents, you have this ontology which is essentially a schema defining how data points can interact with other data points, and then you run the extraction, usually done through an LLM, to produce knowledge graph instances. These instances are actual samples in your database: multiple documents, multiple people extracted from those documents, multiple tasks, episodes, preferences, and so on.

He then covered the two types of knowledge graphs. **RDF** knowledge graphs and **labeled property graphs**. Every graph at its essence is defined as a collection of entity-relationship-entity triplets, but you also want to attach metadata to entities and relationships. In the RDF method, which is older, every piece of metadata is attached as a triplet itself. For example, if you want to attach a source type or a date to a document, each becomes its own triplet. This can cause the knowledge graph to explode in size. That is why **property graphs** exist, where you attach all metadata as JSON objects to the core entity. Property graphs are what is typically used in GraphRAG and in agents.

Abi then posed a question to the class: where would you use GraphRAG? A student named Sean suggested structured data and getting contextual information by hopping across multiple nodes. Abi built on this, explaining that GraphRAG is particularly useful when you have a clearly defined schema and want to identify relationships, and when you need to reduce hallucination in complex queries involving interconnected facts. It becomes especially valuable for legal, medical, or financial documents, or anywhere you are using agentic systems on organizational data where understanding who information comes from and how it relates to different roles matters. She noted it is more expensive to build compared to typical RAG applications, but the advantages are interpretability within the system and having more contextual information, which eventually reduces hallucination.

A student then asked about the construction of the knowledge graph itself. Specifically, whether the LLMs used for extraction are specially trained for this task, and whether they can handle open-domain knowledge graph creation, detecting new classes of entities that were not predefined in the ontology.

Paul broke the answer into two parts. For the type of LLM used in extraction, you typically start with a general-purpose frontier model when you are at the proof-of-concept stage, because you are not yet sure it will work and you want to see results quickly. He mentioned using **Gemini** models, even the Lite version, and from **Claude**, smaller models like Haiku, which with good prompt engineering are sufficient for the beginning. But when you want to scale and process large amounts of data, you want to choose smaller models that are fine-tuned specifically for extracting entities and relationships from your particular type of data.

On the second part of the question, about discovering new entity types beyond the predefined ontology, Paul suggested that open-ended extraction is most useful during the data exploration phase, when you want to understand what type of ontology would make sense for your data and want to extract insights and get inspiration. However, once you move past exploration, *you should stick to your ontology*. If you do not define and adhere to it, the output will quickly become such a mess that it will be useless. This also makes extraction much cheaper, because instead of letting the LLM extract everything, which can be a lot, you reduce it to just the key core entities and relationships you are interested in, making the process sustainable, cheaper, and faster.

Abi added that companies like **Gable** and **Palantir** are using similar approaches, building small GraphRAG systems to extract entities and construct context graphs, particularly in the security and metadata domains.

Paul agreed, noting that wherever you want full control over the reasoning is where you need GraphRAG. He emphasized that GraphRAG is mostly a conceptual approach to how you traverse your data, and the implementation he was presenting is just one way to do it.

He then walked through the extraction methods in more detail. **Structured extraction** is always schema-guided: you have the ontology, and the LLM outputs entities exactly based on that schema. **Semi-structured extraction** is based on metadata and lineage. For example, from the references of a document, you already know it links to other documents, or from the author metadata you can make connections without needing an LLM. **Unstructured extraction** uses an LLM without a schema, relying on prompt engineering, and is mainly useful for discovery. In practice, you combine these methods. For instance, in the digital twin ontology, a person is extracted using the structured method and connected to tasks, episodes, and preferences using the same method, but connected to documents using the semi-structured method. Documents are connected to other documents semi-structurally as well. You can even combine approaches: if a person references another person through a discussion, you use structured extraction via an LLM to mine that relationship.

With those fundamentals in place, Paul transitioned to the hands-on portion: actually building a GraphRAG system. He outlined five big components. First, the **data pipeline**, which gathers data from all your sources, such as URIs from the internet, notes, emails, and normalizes everything into a document data structure stored in a data warehouse. Second, the **memory pipeline**, which takes those documents and outputs knowledge graph objects containing triplets, vectors for semantic search, and other metadata, then writes them to immutable logs. Third, the **unified memory**, where those immutable logs are materialized into a queryable knowledge graph. Fourth, the **knowledge graph** itself. And fifth, the **agent**, which talks to your data, usually through an **MCP server** or similar method. He highlighted the dynamic between short-term and long-term memory: a search memory tool lets the agent bring information from long-term memory into the context window, using only what it needs at that moment, while a write memory tool lets the agent take new insights from a conversation and push them back into the unified memory through similar pipelines. For the database layer, Paul noted there are two core options: you can use a single unified database such as **Postgres** or **MongoDB**, both of which can support GraphRAG.

For this type of application, you do not need to go too crazy. If you connect just two or three nodes, which is called a two-to-three hop, it is often more than enough. You just want to know that a person has a few tasks, and those tasks are also connected to another person. Very often that is sufficient, and Postgres and MongoDB can do this really well. Like this, you have just one piece of infrastructure, just one database, and for most applications out there — because you are most probably not building the next Google, you are building some sort of more scoped application — this would be just good enough. Then, if you want to go specialized and super hardcore into building knowledge graphs at big scale, you can go with specialized graph databases such as **Neo4j**.

A student asked what happens if a particular node is not detected by the community algorithm — if it is an isolated node or an isolated subgraph. Would that be put in an immutable log, and if so, how would you search from it, since it is an isolated graph? Paul said he would answer that question when he explained how **immutable logs** work, since the concepts are somewhat separated, and promised it would make sense.

The **memory pipeline** is a bit more complex than you would expect from a normal RAG ingestion pipeline. You basically take your document, process it, and prepare it for GraphRAG. Usually you have a cleaning step and, optionally, a **chunking** step. To be honest, chunking is very popular in the RAG world, but if you can do it without chunking, Paul recommended doing it without chunking, because it introduces all kinds of issues — though it depends a lot on the data you are working with. Then you have the **graph extractor**, which is the actual LLM or other methods that extract triplets from your unstructured or structured data. After that comes the **normalization** step, which is super important. For example, if you extract the "Abi" entity, it will most probably appear multiple times in your data. The key thing is that you want to track the evolution of that instance from your knowledge graph over time. You do not want to have multiple copies of Abi — you want just one Abi that is constantly updated. In the immutable logs collection, the same ID can appear multiple times. You insert it the first time, and then during normalization you recognize that you already have that entity in your graph, so you put the same ID with some new metadata and new relationships on top of it, and you just update it. Ultimately, there is the **embedding** step, where you can take different parts of your entity and relationship and embed them, depending on what makes sense. You can usually use the content of the chunk, but if you want to go further, you can embed multiple elements from it.

For the graph extraction step specifically, you start with a foundational model. Usually the easiest approach is to use an API, because at the very beginning you just want to see that things work. For the first iteration you just want to kick things off. Yes, it will be more expensive and probably slower, but performance-wise it will most probably work just fine. You can use the smaller or medium-level models from companies like Google or Anthropic, and they will work just fine, especially because they will be guided by the **ontology**. That is where you should actually put more time — into building that ontology and doing **prompt engineering** for how the model should extract each element. For example, with **Pydantic** you can define descriptors for the entity and relationship itself and for each field, and you inject those during extraction. You basically explain to the LLM how it should look for each field, so it does not guess but goes straight to it.

Now let's move to the immutable logs part, which, from what Paul has seen, usually does not come out of the box from most GraphRAG frameworks. He had actually started to dig into a few, and usually they do not use this pattern. Indeed, for small scale you do not need it, but when you go big, you do. Basically, you first have these **immutable logs** where you put your knowledge graph objects. As the name states, you always just append to this collection. You never delete, you never update — it is an append-only collection. The key step is the normalization step, where you append new items but can append items with the same entity or relationship ID. Then, when you actually want to query this data, you have the **materialization** step, where you merge all the entities and relationships into a single entity based on the ID. Like this, you can actually see the evolution of a specific entity and relationship over time. If you merge everything, you have the whole evolution, but you also get versioning out of this, because you can go up to a specific point in time and merge only from that point, or only starting from a specific point. You can slice your data in the time dimension, and you can even soft delete things. Since we work with LLMs here and they fail really often, you need versioning, you need a way to revert if you really have to. So having these immutable logs gives you versioning, temporality, and reversibility. When you merge stuff into a single entity or relationship that will be queryable, you do that based on the ID. And usually this merging is straightforward — just imagine you have multiple dictionaries and you merge those Python dictionaries or JSONs into one big dictionary. It is really nothing crazy.

Coming back to the earlier question about community detection, Paul explained that it works because now we have collections of entities and nodes that actually represent the knowledge graph — just one person, just one document, everything unique. If you have a single entity in a community, that is just fine. These are problems that often come with clustering, and you can further filter them out or do whatever makes sense, but from this point of view it is not really an issue.

A student followed up, asking about compaction: since it is an immutable, append-only log, what happens if the data grows too much or the ETL job is producing too much data? How do you keep appending without any sort of compaction? Paul responded that databases can easily handle millions of records — you should expect hundreds of millions of records depending on the scale of your data. You should design your system to expect that explosion of samples, but each sample will be small, just a few attributes with a small footprint. Even Postgres and MongoDB can easily handle billions of records. The student pressed further, noting that databases can also shard. Paul agreed — the immutable logs can be sharded eventually, since you have IDs that you can partition on, for example partitioning on the entity or relationship ID.

Here is the data model. This example is in **MongoDB**, but it would look very similar in Postgres. For the immutable logs, you have a node and an edge. You can actually keep them under the same collection. For the node, you have the unique ID of the log itself (which usually is not that useful), the type (node or edge), the actual name of the entity, the type from the ontology, and some properties of that specific entity — these properties will be different after each extraction. You can also have lineage information like the source document ID, the chunk ID, and when it was created. For the edge, you again have the unique ID of the log and the type. Then you define the triplet: the source of this relationship is one node with a given type, and the target is another node with its type. Then you have the type of the actual edge. For example, an edge might define that the node "Paul Iusztin" is author of "Decoding AI." You can also attach properties on the relationships themselves.

When you materialize this information, you squash multiple logs into a single entity. All the accumulated properties appear, and you also compute the **embedding**, which is often done during materialization because it is super hard to compute an embedding on an object that does not have its full state. You can also retain the IDs linking back to all the logs for lineage, allowing you to understand where the data comes from. The same operation applies to edges. The key point is that you end up with just two collections in your database: the immutable logs collection and the materialized knowledge graph collection. Especially for the knowledge graph collection, you need this because you want to do recursive traversals over your graph. In MongoDB, for example, there is built-in support for following IDs within a collection to do multi-hops automatically from a query, or with SQL you can do the same with joins.

Now let's see how this gets combined with normal RAG. For normal RAG, you always have text search and sometimes semantic search — RAG does not always necessarily come with semantic search if you do not need it. For GraphRAG, we combine **text search**, **semantic search**, and **graph search** based on this new type of modeling. These are the three indexes, and you can also add **communities**, which are not necessarily indexes per se but also a way to help search information more easily.

As a quick recap: text search allows you to search exact keywords in your data. For example, if you search "vector databases," it will find those exact keywords. Semantic search performs really badly on exact keywords, but it is really good at finding semantic similarity — sentences with the same meaning but different keywords. For example, "embedding stores" is similar to vector database tools like FAISS or Pinecone, or "prefers Qdrant." These connections can also be between different types of entities, and you can start creating rich connections. The third type of index is graph search — the graph hops that were just discussed — where you can query what an entity is connected to and what other types of entities surround it.

A student asked about merging the results from text search, semantic search, and graph search: is there a known way to prioritize each result in re-ranking, or does it depend on the data and how you weight results? Is it always that graph search results should re-rank the semantic search, which re-ranks the text search? Paul said it was a great question. Usually, you first do a normal RAG search using text search and semantic search to grab your first nodes — your **entry points** into the knowledge graph. That is exactly how RAG works. You typically do that with **RRF** (Reciprocal Rank Fusion), where you query your text index and your semantic search index and merge the results based on frequency. You do a cutoff — for example, if multiple text search and semantic search queries hit the same node, that is a clear signal you should keep it. Then, based on those initial hits from text and semantic search, you start doing the graph traversals.

For the retrieval part, there are two options. The more classic and most commonly used one is the **bottom-up format**. First you do text and semantic search to get your first set of entities — your entry points into the graph. Then you start expanding your set of entities using the relationships within your graph, doing two or three multi-hops. Optionally, if you have communities layered on top of your data, you can see which communities all your entities belong to, and then return both your entities and communities. You package all of these to your LLM and generate an answer, similar to any other RAG system. What Paul wanted to highlight is that *GraphRAG, different from normal RAG, in reality is just this multi-hop traversal step during retrieval.* That is what it adds, along with all the data modeling that was presented.

The second option is the **top-down format**, used when you are not that interested in all the details but want to quickly find a high-level overview of a specific topic. Here again you use text and semantic search to get your entry points into the graph, but instead of retrieving entity nodes, you retrieve only the communities. Then you multi-hop through the communities using bridge nodes. You retrieve only a set of communities, and based on the summaries of those communities, you generate an answer using the LLM. So you get only a very high-level overview of your clusters, instead of the bottom-up option where you get depth on specific nodes and the exact details from each.

The last step is to give the agent access to this knowledge graph. This is basically **agentic GraphRAG**. Up to this point, Paul had described only a GraphRAG system. By giving an agent access to write or search this GraphRAG, now you have an agentic GraphRAG system. You can serve the write-memory and search-memory tools through an **MCP** server, though you can also do this through a normal FastAPI server, or if the agent is baked directly on your server you can just do simple queries. There is a lot of freedom in how you want these tools to look, but conceptually, the idea is that you want your agents to dynamically compute queries talking to your GraphRAG. For example, if you use **Cypher**, you want your agent to dynamically write Cypher code to query your knowledge graph based on the conversation, or to write SQL code, or MongoDB queries — whatever your query language is. During write operations, you can bake in the memory pipeline with a slight change: instead of getting a document as input, you have the actual conversation as input, and you run the same steps to write new information into your immutable logs. Like this, you can extract insights from the conversation on top of your external data. And this is basically one type of memory — this is why it is called a **unified memory**.

To give a concrete example of how all these pieces come together, consider the query "create a presentation on GraphRAG for O'Reilly, for engineers." This is the input to the agent. First, it goes and uses text search and semantic search to find everything it can. It finds entities on these topics, then it merges these entry points using different techniques. **RRF** is probably the most common and simplest one to merge searches from multiple indexes. That gives you your first input into the graph.

From there, say you have a hit on **Arthur** as a person. You can start seeing, based on these relationships, that Arthur actually did an O'Reilly episode in 2023, had another graph representation, had some preferences on how visuals should look. You can expand from there as well. You might find other documents connected to those visuals, documents from your branding, materials from this O'Reilly presentation, other documents that were used to create that episode, and so on.

After you do this multi-hop traversal, you typically get your communities, for example your GraphRAG research community and your O'Reilly events community. You package all of that up into an LLM and it gets all the context it needs from your memory to complete the task.

As a specific use case, for medical issues, or legal matters, it is super important to have these connections. But personally, I think also for personal assistant types of applications, for example, a personal assistant that needs to make connections between all these silos you have for yourself, between your emails, your notes, your research, your CRM, your contacts, to actually make connections and create plans, tasks, to-do lists, and manage your time schedule. You need GraphRAG for that. The beauty of it is that the scale is not that huge, because you as a person probably do not have millions of documents. You might have thousands. Over the course of your lifespan, you might have tens of thousands of documents. But it is a small to medium data problem that can very beautifully be solved with this approach.

The second example is a more general one. Let's say we want to ask our system, "When and where should I go with my cats to the vet?" This is more of a task management example, where our personal assistant knows what we should do. We hit with text search and remember that we have some SMSs, some Notion documents, some Google Drive documents. With semantic search, we get similar things from our emails, our to-do app, our preferences stored in our notes.

Based on this, we can start with multi-hops and get an episode that reflects past experiences. We remember that one of the cats had a bad reaction to a specific medicine. We also have a task that we should ask Felix for a specific vet number. The idea is that we can find specific connections: from this email I created this task, and it is particularly connected to some previous tasks based on that particular experience. With semantic search or text search alone, this is very hard to achieve, especially because during retrieval you need to do aggressive filtering and cutoff, where you might get what you need but you might not. Imagine what would happen if, in this particular use case, you missed the fact that one of the cats had a bad reaction to a specific vaccine, and then you want that baked into your recommendations and reminders for the vet visit. By doing this graph traversal, the probability of actually missing that connection is a lot lower.

For those who want to go deeper, he publishes weekly content on his **Decoding AI** magazine, covering topics like AI agents, orchestration, and related subjects, all for free on a weekly basis. He also mentioned his freshly launched **Agentic AI Engineering** course, which he finished about a month ago. It is self-paced, you buy it once, and you can do it whenever you want.

Abi thanked Paul for the fantastic presentation and turned to questions from the audience. A student asked about optimizing retrieval and maintaining accuracy as the graph keeps growing.

Paul acknowledged it was a good question and was candid that there is no single concrete answer, since every dataset is unique. If your ontology is clean enough, that should be sufficient at the schema level. But at the data level, you probably need to build cleaning routines. The key is to look at your data. Usually, after you build this system, you need an evals layer where you are forced to examine your data and understand how it actually looks. When you do that, you start seeing patterns and what goes wrong. A good practice is to extract rules on what good data looks like and what bad data looks like, and based on that, continuously update your ontology and your extraction mechanisms. You can also build housekeeping pipelines. Because you have that versioning layer built with immutable logs, it is extremely easy to build a classifier using insights from examining your data, soft-delete the problematic entries, rerun your materialization, and you are back on track. If someone later says that an item was actually important and the housekeeping pipeline messed up, you can revert that as well. Keeping your data clean, Paul noted, is probably the most complicated part of all of this.

A student then asked about creating the *minimum viable context*, the final context result fed into the LLM to answer user queries. Specifically, is it useful to use a smaller, specialized LLM for summarization or restructuring, so the main LLM is not overwhelmed by the retrieved context?

Paul said it was a great question and that imagination is the limit. What the student described is one option, but if the chunks are not that large, or you only want the top ten or twenty chunks, it might be enough to throw everything in with nice formatting such as YAML. Alternatively, you can use another LLM to extract and summarize, or you can use re-ranking logic to see what is more relevant to your query. For example, if you have retrieved a hundred chunks and want to pass down only the first twenty, you can use re-ranking to reprioritize them relative to your query. His opinion is that there is a lot of room for improvement here, but the best approach is to start super simple: keep the top-K elements at a medium size, not too small, build an evals layer, understand what you are actually trying to optimize for, and then try increasing top-K, or adding sorting based on the semantic search score, which is usually not the smartest approach but might work, and then layer on re-ranking, summarization, and more complexity. Maybe you do not care that much about latency, maybe the costs are not that big, so maybe you can just pass in a hundred thousand tokens and that is fine for your use case. That is often the case.

The same student followed up, asking about using a needle-in-the-haystack test to evaluate whether the RAG system is good enough. Paul explained that for RAG problems, there are really only two types of metrics. First, there are retrieval metrics, where you test how well your retrieval works using information retrieval metrics such as precision and recall on how many of the expected items you actually retrieved, or ranking metrics to see if the first expected item was actually retrieved. The metrics themselves are easy to build because they do not require any fancy LLMs. The hardest part is building the dataset, having pairs of specific queries mapped to specific expected items. What you usually do is take your existing chunks, extract particular queries from those chunks, generate an answer, and then you have your dataset: for a given query, you expect to retrieve these particular chunks and you expect this particular answer.

Then, on the whole system level, regardless of whether it is simple text search, RAG, or GraphRAG, there are really just six types of metrics. You have your question, your answer, and your retrieved context. If you do the combinations between these three, you get six types of metrics: how well the answer relates to the context, how well it relates to the question, how well the context relates to the answer and the question, and so on. This way, you can detect hallucinations, check if the answer is actually useful, and similar concerns. With **LLM judges**, it is not that complicated to build this from scratch. Paul mentioned he had just written an article on this topic and offered to share the link.

Another student asked about graph updates when new documents are added. Specifically, does the whole graph need to be updated, and how long does that take? Paul clarified that it does not need to be a full recomputation. You can do partial materializations, updating just the new IDs that came in. For example, if you extract data from one document, you update just that piece in the query view. When the student pressed further, asking whether the knowledge graph itself needs to be updated, Paul confirmed that yes, but you update just the parts that changed. If you get a new log on person one, you just re-squash the immutable logs that create the state of person one and leave the rest intact. You do not recompute the whole knowledge graph.

Paul added that another option is to have the knowledge graph as a view. If embedding models are not critical, or you are okay computing on partial states, you can have a non-materialized view that is automatically updated because it is virtual, like views in SQL. Again, it depends on what design decisions you make based on your application.

The student then asked a follow-up about race conditions: what happens if the knowledge graph is updating while the agent is simultaneously trying to query it? Paul explained that the database handles these race conditions. The write and search operations are bottlenecked by the database, and databases like Postgres and MongoDB have **ACID** properties that manage this. The worst case that can happen is that you get a slightly stale version of an entity or node, not the absolute freshest version. The student suggested they probably use something like **MVCC**, keeping multiple versions of the same graph so that while the graph is updating, they show the old version and switch to the new one once the update completes. Paul agreed that this made sense, as that is how most databases handle it.

Abi wrapped up the session, thanking Paul for the excellent presentation and noting that this is not something they cover extensively in the course, making it especially interesting. She highlighted that it is one of the more recent approaches and that many companies are moving in this direction. Paul agreed, adding that many questions in this space are hard to answer because the strategies have not been widely benchmarked yet.
